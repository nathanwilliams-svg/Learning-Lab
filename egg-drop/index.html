Egg Drop Designer

Build around the egg in 3D. Add parts from the bin, then click a part to move or turn it. Where you put each part matters. When you're ready, predict and drop.

GuideRead this first. Tap to hide or show.
  1. Add parts. Click a part in the Parts bin. It appears in a starting spot near the egg. Click the same part again to add more. Many parts arrange themselves: six cardboard panels make a box, and cotton balls stack under the egg.
  2. Select a part. Click it in the 3D view. It glows blue, and its name shows in the Selected part panel. Click empty space or press Esc to deselect.
  3. Move it. In Move mode, drag an arrow: red is left/right, green is down/up, blue is back/front. For small, exact moves, use the arrow buttons in the Selected part panel.
  4. Turn it. Switch to Turn mode and drag a colored ring, or use the Tip and Spin buttons. Check Big steps to turn 90° at a time, which is handy for standing a panel up.
  5. Look around. Drag empty space to rotate the view. Scroll or pinch to zoom. Use Front, Side and Top to check that parts line up.
  6. Add tape. Tape holds everything together. The design check tells you how many strips your build needs.
  7. Fix the design check. Red and orange warnings show problems. Cushion on each side shows how much protection the egg has in every direction, and Balance shows whether it will land upright.
  8. Predict and drop. Name your egg, pick where to drop from and how high, make a prediction, and press Drop it! Watch the instant replay, then try the other replay angles. Press Back to build, improve one thing, and drop again. Change one variable at a time so you know what made the difference.
  9. Earn stars and badges. Cheap, light designs that survive earn more stars. Check Challenges & badges for goals, and share your best drop on the Class leaderboard.
  10. Save and turn in. Save each version under Saved designs. See the Turning in your data tab for how to get your trial log into Google Sheets.

The rules

  • Parts join when they touch. Every part must touch the egg, or touch a part that touches the egg, in an unbroken chain. A part that isn't connected turns red, because it would fall off.
  • Check two views. A part can look like it touches in Front view but be behind the build. Check Front and Side views before you test.
  • Tape holds it all. Bigger builds need more tape. Without enough, parts shift and the cushioning works less well.
  • Keep it upright, or protect every side. A package with no heavy bottom tumbles and lands on its weakest side. Put heavy parts like cardboard low, at least 3 cm below the egg's center, and Balance will say it lands upright.

Combining parts into one piece

  1. Pick the parts. Turn on Pick several in the 3D view, or hold Shift, and click each part you want.
  2. Combine them. Press Combine into one piece (or C). Clicking any of those parts now selects the whole piece, so you can move, turn, copy or delete it at once.
  3. Break it apart. Press Separate to make each part move on its own again.

Combining is your choice and only makes parts move together. Parts in a piece still have to touch to count.

Ways to combine materials

  • Nest: set the egg in a cup and fill the gaps with cotton or newspaper.
  • Layer: only material between the egg and the ground cushions it. Put the most padding on the side it lands on.
  • Crumple legs: straws standing on end under a cardboard plate crumple on impact and protect the whole package. More straws work better. Straws lying flat barely help.
  • Weighted base: stack cardboard under the build so it's bottom-heavy and lands upright.
  • Box: a cardboard box needs padding inside to connect the egg to the walls, or the egg rattles loose.
  • Bumpers: balloons on every side protect the egg no matter how it lands.
  • Suspend: rubber bands around the egg work only with a frame to stretch across (2+ cardboard panels or 8+ straws).

Copy your trial log into Google Sheets

  1. Type your name in Your name at the top before you start dropping, so every trial is labeled.
  2. Press Copy for Google Sheets under the Trial log. You'll see "Copied" when it worked.
  3. Open your sheet. Use the sheet your teacher gave you, or open a new one from Google Drive or by typing sheets.new in the address bar.
  4. Click cell A1 (or the first empty row), then paste with Ctrl+V. On a Mac, use ⌘+V. Each value lands in its own column.
  5. Make a graph if your teacher asks: select two columns, like Height and Force on egg, then choose Insert › Chart.

    The science behind the drop

    1. Speed builds up

    Gravity speeds the package up the whole way down, so a higher drop means a faster hit. Air pushes back a little on big, light shapes, but most of the protection has to come from stopping gently.

    2. Stop it gently

    Only the material between the egg and the ground can cushion it. Padding, crumple zones, balloons and rubber-band suspension stretch out the stop. Packages that aren't bottom-heavy can tumble and land on any side.

    3. The force on the egg

    The simulator estimates the average force on the egg as it stops:

    Force = egg mass × speed² ÷ (2 × stopping distance)

    In this lab a hard-boiled egg cracks at about 35 N. Its solid inside makes it tougher than a raw egg, but the shell still cracks.

    Teacher settings

    Settings are stored on this device only. How the model works: parts must touch the build (within 1 cm) to count. Each side's cushion is measured along five lines out from the egg, using how squishy each material is and which way it faces (a straw standing on end crumples; one lying flat barely helps). Rubber bands near the egg add suspension when there's a frame. A heavy bottom (center of mass at least 3 cm below the egg's center) keeps it upright; otherwise it lands on its weakest side. Straws and cardboard count across the whole build, since they carry the load for the whole package. The egg is hard-boiled: it cracks at the toughness setting and is smashed at 1.75 times that force.
